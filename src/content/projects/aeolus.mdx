# Overview

Aeolus is a thrust-vectored electric ducted fan (EDF) rocket built to hover and fly under closed-loop control. Unlike a solid motor, an EDF throttles continuously — which buys controlled ascent, hover, and landing without the field clearance a solid motor demands. It also removes the one thing that normally keeps a rocket pointing where it is going.

**At hover, airspeed is zero.** Fins need airflow to generate a restoring moment, so below a few metres per second they do nothing at all. The vehicle is aerodynamically unstable from the moment it leaves the ground, and at hover it never picks up the speed that would fix that.

**The only thing holding it upright is four vanes in the EDF exhaust**, driven by a control loop running at 200 Hz on hardware I built. That demands an attitude estimate good to a fraction of a degree, gains matched to the real vehicle rather than guessed, and enough measured control authority to know the vanes can actually win. Each of those is a page in this section.

Every subsystem was designed and built from scratch: the airframe and CAD, three custom PCBs, the flight firmware, the sensor fusion and control laws, CFD and a 6DOF Simulink model, the LoRa link and browser ground station, and the load-cell test stand used to characterize it.

# Theory Meets Reality

Most hobby PID tuning is trial and error. Because I had independently measured both the vehicle's inertia — by [bifilar pendulum](/projects/aeolus/vehicle#moment-of-inertia-testing) — and the vanes' torque per degree, I could treat each axis as a damped harmonic oscillator and *solve* for the gains instead of guessing them:

$$I\ddot{\theta} + K_d\dot{\theta} + K_p\theta = 0$$

Picking a closed-loop natural frequency $\omega$ and damping ratio $\zeta$ then fixes both gains directly, converted from physical torque into commanded vane degrees:

$$K_p = \frac{I\omega^2}{\tau/\text{deg}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180} \qquad\qquad K_d = \frac{2I\zeta\omega}{\tau/\text{deg}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}$$

Targeting $\omega = 4$ rad/s and $\zeta = 0.85$ on roll produced gains that worked on the first attempt. The flown configuration corresponds to $\zeta \approx 0.83$, and the measured roll response tracked the model closely. Pitch and yaw, tuned the same way for $\zeta = 0.8$, came out slightly *less* damped than predicted in flight — but the trim angles the model predicted were spot on.

<figure class="not-prose my-6 mx-auto max-w-xl">
  <img src="/assets/aeolus/control/roll-response.webp" alt="Measured roll response with the derived gains" class="w-full rounded-lg" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption class="mt-2 text-[11px] text-slate-500 dark:text-slate-400 text-center">Roll response flown with the derived gains.</figcaption>
</figure>

The same reasoning is what told me not to reuse the method on the velocity loop. There, acceleration is the *first* derivative of the controlled variable rather than the second, so the loop is first-order: oscillation is impossible and the response is a pure exponential, $v = v_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = (K_d + 1)/K_p$. Those gains came from a target settling time instead of a damping ratio. Knowing when a method stops applying mattered more than the derivation itself.

# Status

*Updated 31 August 2026.*

**Aeolus flies.** Roll, pitch, and yaw control were isolated and validated on a tether through July. The vanes were enlarged in early August and each axis re-characterized against the new geometry. The horizontal velocity loop flew on 21 August, and the first untethered flight followed on 22 August. Position hold was tested on 27 August and ended in the vehicle's first crash — hard enough to be worth recording, gentle enough that inspection found no damage.

Three things are open. A 3.0 Hz roll limit cycle appears in free flight and in no tethered test. RPM sensor interference produced an oscillating throttle on one flight. And heading initialization is unsolved, so it currently flies without the magnetometer.
