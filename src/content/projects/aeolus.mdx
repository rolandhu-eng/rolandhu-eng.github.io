# Overview

Aeolus is a thrust-vectored electric ducted fan (EDF) rocket built to hover and fly under active control. Unlike a solid motor, an EDF throttles continuously — which buys controlled ascent, hover, and landing without the field clearance a solid motor demands. It also combines two different control systems to provide authority during both hover and engine-off coast.

At hover, airspeed is zero. Fins need airflow to generate a restoring moment, so below a few meters per second they do nothing at all. That is why I also included vanes in the exhaust. The angle of both the external fins and the internal vanes can be steered, enabling active control. This system generates a control moment from two sources:

$$\frac{\tau}{\text{deg}} \;=\; \underbrace{K_{vane} \cdot T}_{\text{exhaust thrust vectoring}} \;+\; \underbrace{K_{fin} \cdot \bar{q}}_{\text{fins in freestream air}}$$

The vanes work whenever the fan is spinning. The fins work whenever the vehicle is moving. Between them there is *almost* no part of the envelope — hover, climb, or unpowered fall — where the vehicle has nothing to steer with. The only exception is engine relight in base-first descent-the exhaust flow and external airflow are opposed, so the vanes and fins sharing a shaft will produce opposing control moments. The [allocator](/projects/aeolus/control#control-allocation) has to decide which effector is winning before it can decide which way to deflect.

All of this demands precise gain tuning, modelling, simulation, attitude estimates, and more—each of those is a page here.

Every subsystem was designed and built from scratch: the airframe and CAD, two custom PCBs, the flight firmware, the sensor fusion and control architecture, CFD and a 6DOF Simulink model, the LoRa link and browser ground station, and the load-cell test stand used to characterize it.

As of September 2026 it flies the whole profile on its own — launch, climb, hover, drop and land, with no input after the launch command. The most recent attempt, at 40 m, was pushed downwind and struck a fence when the barometer read several metres low on the way down; the controller was working correctly and solving against the wrong altitude. [The teardown is on the testing page](/projects/aeolus/testing#5-september--30-m-clean-40-m-into-a-fence).

---

Aeolus is a solo project. I designed it, built it, wrote the software, and flew it.

**Designed and fabricated.** The airframe and full CAD assembly, the four-vane thrust vector linkage, the nose fairing and its generatively designed hinges. Two custom PCBs — power distribution and servo distribution — laid out in EasyEDA, ordered, and hand-soldered including the SMD work. The flight computer is a perfboard build around a Teensy 4.1. I also designed and built the four-bar load cell test stand, with its own firmware and radio link, to characterize the vehicle.

**Wrote.** All flight firmware, in C++: the Madgwick attitude filter re-derived for an X-up body frame, complementary and Kalman fusion for altitude and velocity, the cascaded position/velocity/attitude controllers, the control allocator, the RPM governor, the flight state machine that sequences the mission, and the PSRAM-to-flash datalogger. Also the LoRa command protocol, the ESP32 ground station firmware, and the browser dashboard it talks to over Web Serial.

**Measured and analyzed.** Inertia by bifilar pendulum. Vane control authority two independent ways — on the test stand, and again by fitting flight logs. Thrust, current and RPM across the throttle range. Accelerometer and magnetometer calibration by ellipsoid fit. GPS velocity noise characterization. Every flight log analyzed in Python.

**Modelled.** CFD in ANSYS Fluent for the vane and intake aerodynamics, and a 6DOF flight model in MATLAB/Simulink driven by those coefficients and my measured mass properties.

The vehicle is 1.444 kg and about a metre long. Everything above the intake is payload in the sense that it does no work in flight — it is there to be carried, and because it sits furthest from the centre of mass it is the mass that costs the most to rotate. Everything below the intake does the flying.

# Contents

**1. [Vehicle](/projects/aeolus/vehicle)** — How it is built and the structural design, and the planned and characterized but currently unused parachute recovery system. Also, how I measured the moment of inertia with a bi-filar pendulum setup, which the rest of the control and software was based around.

**2. [Propulsion](/projects/aeolus/propulsion)** — The ducted fan, what it actually produces on a test stand rather than the stated specs, and the software that holds it at a commanded *speed* — because as the battery drains, the same throttle stops meaning the same thrust.

**3. [Avionics](/projects/aeolus/avionics)** — The electronics, designed and soldered by hand. The flight computer and two custom PCBs: a power distribution board and a simple servo distribution board.

**4. [GNC](/projects/aeolus/gnc)** — How it works out its current orientation and movement, to a fraction of a degree, because a one-degree error becomes eight meters of position error in ten seconds. Also the state machine that flies the mission unaided.

**5. [Control](/projects/aeolus/control)** — How it corrects for disturbances and commands 200 times per second, and how it physically does that. Also how the vehicle response is modelled to solve for optimal gains analytically, compared to the common trial-and-error tuning.

**6. [Simulation](/projects/aeolus/simulation)** — Airflow simulation and a six-degree-of-freedom flight model, used to simulate software settings before real flight. Predicts what the vehicle will behave like before actually flying.

**7. [Communications](/projects/aeolus/communications)** — Radio link, command protocol, flight recorder, and the browser dashboard I fly from. How adding two fields to the log record made the flight computer hard fault on every single write.

**8. [Testing](/projects/aeolus/testing)** — Every test and every flight, failures included and documented as thoroughly as the successes — including a 3 Hz roll oscillation that only appears untethered.
