Aeolus is a rocket-shaped vehicle that flies and lands itself. Instead of a rocket motor it uses an electric ducted fan, a motor-driven fan inside a tube, which can be throttled up and down. That lets it take off, hover in place and come back down to land, in a backyard instead of at a launch field.

Nothing about it is passively stable. When it hovers there is no airflow over its fins, so the only thing keeping it upright is a flight computer that reads its sensors and moves four small vanes in the fan's exhaust 200 times a second. I designed and built every part of it: the airframe, the circuit boards, the flight software, and the test stand used to measure it.

In September 2026 it flew a 30 m flight entirely on its own: launch, climb, hover, descend and land, with no input after I pressed launch. The next attempt, at 40 m in strong wind, ended in a fence. The barometer read several metres low on the way down, so the vehicle flew a correct landing toward ground that was not there yet. The controller did its job with a wrong measurement, though in that wind it also drifted and held its attitude only roughly. The lesson I took from it is that a control system is only as good as the sensors it trusts. [What happened, in detail](/projects/aeolus/testing#5-september--30-m-clean-40-m-into-a-fence).

# How It Steers

At hover, airspeed is zero. Fins need airflow to generate a restoring moment, so below a few meters per second they do nothing at all. That is why I also included vanes in the exhaust. The angle of both the external fins and the internal vanes can be steered, enabling active control. This system generates a control moment from two sources:

$$
\frac{\tau}{\text{deg}} \;=\; \underbrace{K_{vane} \cdot T}_{\text{exhaust thrust vectoring}} \;+\; \underbrace{K_{fin} \cdot \bar{q}}_{\text{fins in freestream air}}
$$

The vanes work whenever the fan is spinning. The fins work whenever the vehicle is moving. Between them there is *almost* no phase of flight — hover, climb, or unpowered fall — where the vehicle has nothing to steer with. The exception is restarting the fan while falling tail-first: the exhaust and the oncoming air push in opposite directions, so the linked vanes and fins produce opposing moments, and the [allocator](/projects/aeolus/control#control-allocation) has to work out which one is winning before it can decide which way to deflect.

<figure class="not-prose my-6 mx-auto max-w-sm">
  <img src="/assets/aeolus/vehicle/aft-end-vanes-fins.webp" alt="The aft end of Aeolus, looking into the exhaust: four vanes inside the duct and two of the fins outside" class="w-full rounded-lg" loading="lazy" decoding="async"  width="1787" height="2000" />
  <figcaption class="mt-2 text-[11px] text-slate-500 dark:text-slate-400 text-center">Looking into the exhaust: the four vanes sit inside the duct, and the fins sit outside.</figcaption>
</figure>

So far the vanes have done almost all the work. At the speeds flown (around 5 m/s), the fins produce under 0.5% of the vane moment, so the fin half of the design has [not yet been tested in flight](/projects/aeolus/control#what-is-still-untested).

All of this demands precise gain tuning, modelling, simulation, attitude estimates, and more—each of those is a page here.

<Dropdown title="For engineers: what I designed, wrote, measured and modelled">

**Designed and fabricated.** The airframe and full CAD assembly, the four-vane thrust vector linkage, the nose fairing and its generatively designed hinges. Two custom PCBs — power distribution and servo distribution — laid out in EasyEDA, ordered, and hand-soldered including the SMD work. The flight computer is a perfboard build around a Teensy 4.1. I also designed and built the four-bar load cell test stand, with its own firmware and radio link, to characterize the vehicle.

**Wrote.** All flight firmware, in C++:
- the [Madgwick attitude filter](/projects/aeolus/gnc#madgwick-filter), which keeps the gyro's drifting orientation estimate honest using the accelerometer's sense of which way is down, re-derived for an X-up body frame
- [complementary](/projects/aeolus/gnc#vertical-channel) and [Kalman](/projects/aeolus/gnc#gps-fusion) fusion for altitude and velocity, each pairing a smooth sensor that drifts with a noisy one that doesn't
- the [cascaded position, velocity and attitude controllers](/projects/aeolus/control#loop-structure), each loop feeding a setpoint to a faster one inside it
- the [control allocator](/projects/aeolus/control#control-allocation), which turns "rotate this way" into four servo angles
- the [RPM governor](/projects/aeolus/propulsion#rpm-governor), which holds the fan at a commanded speed as the battery voltage drops
- the [flight state machine](/projects/aeolus/gnc#flight-sequencing) that sequences the mission, and the [PSRAM-to-flash datalogger](/projects/aeolus/communications#datalogging)

Also the [LoRa command protocol](/projects/aeolus/communications#protocol-design), the ESP32 ground station firmware, and the [browser dashboard](/projects/aeolus/communications#ground-station) it talks to over Web Serial.

**Measured and analyzed.** Inertia by [bifilar pendulum](/projects/aeolus/vehicle#bi-filar-pendulum-setup): hang the vehicle from two strings, time how fast it twists back and forth, and work out how hard it is to rotate. [Vane control authority](/projects/aeolus/control#measuring-control-authority), the turning force one degree of vane produces, two independent ways: on the test stand, and again by fitting flight logs (the two disagree by about 3.5×, which is still unexplained). [Thrust, current and RPM](/projects/aeolus/propulsion#thrust-characterization) across the throttle range. [Accelerometer and magnetometer calibration](/projects/aeolus/avionics#sensor-calibration) by ellipsoid fit, which corrects each sensor's offset and scale errors. [GPS velocity noise](/projects/aeolus/avionics#gps-characterization) characterization. Every flight log analyzed in Python.

**Modelled.** [CFD](/projects/aeolus/simulation#cfd) (simulated airflow) in ANSYS Fluent for the vane and intake aerodynamics, and a [6DOF flight model](/projects/aeolus/simulation#simulink), three directions of movement plus three axes of rotation, in MATLAB/Simulink driven by those coefficients and my measured mass properties.

# Contents

**1. [Flight Testing](/projects/aeolus/testing)** — Probably every test and every flight, failures, successes, and analysis documented.

**2. [Vehicle](/projects/aeolus/vehicle)** — How it is built and the structural design, and how I measured the moment of inertia with a bi-filar pendulum setup, which the rest of the control and software was based around. Also the parachute recovery system, which is designed and characterized but not currently flown.

**3. [Propulsion](/projects/aeolus/propulsion)** — The ducted fan, what it actually produces on a test stand rather than the stated specs, and the software that holds it at a commanded *speed* — because as the battery drains, the same throttle stops meaning the same thrust.

**4. [Avionics](/projects/aeolus/avionics)** — The electronics, designed and soldered by hand. The flight computer and two custom PCBs: a power distribution board and a simple servo distribution board.

**5. [Navigation & Guidance](/projects/aeolus/gnc)** — How it works out its current orientation and movement, to a fraction of a degree, because a one-degree error becomes eight meters of position error in ten seconds. Also the state machine that flies the mission unaided.

**6. [Control](/projects/aeolus/control)** — How it corrects for disturbances and commands 200 times per second, and how it physically does that. Also how the vehicle response is modelled to solve for optimal gains analytically, compared to the common trial-and-error tuning.

**7. [Simulation](/projects/aeolus/simulation)** — Airflow simulation and a six-degree-of-freedom flight model, used to simulate software settings before real flight. Predicts what the vehicle will behave like before actually flying.

**8. [Communications](/projects/aeolus/communications)** — Radio link, command protocol, flight recorder, and the browser dashboard I fly from.

**9. [Build Log](/projects/aeolus/log)** — The working notebook, entry by entry, as I wrote it during the build.
