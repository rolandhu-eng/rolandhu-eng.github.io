# The Brief

This started in my freshman Introduction to Engineering Design class, as a semester project for a team of three: me, Kye Liang and Eric Gorokhovsky. I was the project manager.

The problem we set ourselves:

> Many aspiring STEM students who may be interested in aerodynamics do not have a way to learn and study it, especially aerodynamic forces and airflow around objects. Our goal was to design and develop a tool that can be used to understand aerodynamics in an intuitive and engaging way. It should be affordable and accessible, and its operation should not be extremely complicated.

The constraints were to finish by 1 May 2025, spend under $125, and use real physics and engineering principles.

We started from five ideas — a water wheel generator, a Rube Goldberg machine, a volumetric LED display, a drone that drives as a car, and a wind tunnel — and scored them in a decision matrix for cost, complexity, use of 3D printing and whether we could finish in time. The volumetric display and the drone were too complex for the time we had; the wind tunnel won.

# Design

Our research pointed us to a subsonic open-return tunnel, the simplest kind used for scale testing: a tube with a fan at the end that pulls air in from the room and lets it back out. A wide inlet contraction narrows into the test section, speeding the air up, and the fan sits at the downstream end, pulling rather than pushing, so the air reaching the model is not churned up by the blades.

Our wind tunnel uses foam board and acrylic windows for the main tunnel section with a 3D printed adapter to connect the fan. Straws were used to straighten the airflow, which ensures the incoming air flows straight through the tunnel with minimal turbulence. For the test stand, we used two load cells to measure force and a servo motor to control the angle of the test object. These were connected to an Arduino, allowing us to control and visualize the forces on the object. A smoke generator made the airflow visible.

# Physics

The wind tunnel is in essence a physics research tool: it studies the motion of air and the forces it puts on objects. Two load cells are fixed perpendicular to each other, so lift and drag are measured separately. By changing the fan speed and the angle of the test object, we can see how those forces change, plotted live on a computer by the Arduino. The smoke trails make flow phenomena like flow separation visible. The electronics — the Arduino, servo motor, load cells and potentiometers for input — are circuits in their own right.

The airspeed in the test section comes from continuity of flow. Well below 100 m/s air is effectively incompressible, and the tunnel has one inlet and one outlet, so the volumetric flow rate $Q$ is the same through every cross-section:

$$
Av = Q = \text{constant} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{Q}{A}
$$

Knowing the fan's flow rate and the area of the test section gives the airspeed there. With the speed known, the measured forces give the lift and drag coefficients $C_L$ and $C_D$ of the test object. The fan is rated at 623 cubic feet per minute, which this formula puts at 13 m/s in the test section. For the research I [measured it instead](/projects/wind-tunnel/upgrading#measuring-airspeed), and it was lower.

# What I Would Change

The tunnel worked, but two things fell short. The airflow was too fast even on the lowest fan setting, so the air in the test section was turbulent enough to scatter the smoke and leave it faint. And attaching every straw by hand took a significant amount of time.

I learned how to use load cells to measure forces and plot them live with an Arduino, how to use the laser cutter, and a lot more 3D modelling and printing, since almost every joint in the tunnel is a printed part. If I built it again I would use a fine honeycomb mesh instead of individual straws, and a larger smoke generator with a higher output.

A year later I came back to the tunnel for research, and fixed what it would need to make real measurements. That is [the next chapter](/projects/wind-tunnel/upgrading).
