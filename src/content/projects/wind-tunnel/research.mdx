# The Question

When air flows over a wing, the layer touching the surface is slowed to a stop by friction. This thin region, the boundary layer, matters far more than its size suggests. Over the top of the wing the pressure rises again toward the trailing edge, and that rising pressure pushes back against the slow air near the surface. At a high enough angle of attack the boundary layer loses all its forward momentum and peels away from the wing. That is flow separation, and when it reaches far enough forward the wing stalls: lift collapses and drag jumps.

Aircraft fight this with flaps and slats, which add weight and moving parts. An electroaerodynamic (EAD) actuator is a possible alternative with none. A high-voltage wire on the wing ionizes the air around it, and the electric field pulls those ions toward a grounded electrode further back. They collide with neutral air on the way and drag it along: an ionic wind of around a metre per second, right where the boundary layer needs the energy.

Most published work on these actuators studies how the flow moves, with smoke or flow-field measurements. I wanted to know what it does to the forces. My hypothesis was that the actuator would increase lift, reduce drag, and push stall to a higher angle.

---

# The Actuator

The wing is a NACA 4412, a well-documented airfoil used on light aircraft, 3D printed in PLA with an 80 mm chord and a 140 mm span, nearly the full width of the test section. The emitter is a 0.15 mm copper wire near the leading edge, connected to the positive side of a 40 kV DC supply. The collector is a 1.8 mm brass tube further back along the chord, on the negative side. When powered, a corona discharge glows along the whole span.

## Electrode Spacing

The gap between the two electrodes decides how strong the ionic wind is. Too wide and the field is weak; too narrow and the air breaks down into sparks. Before any wind tunnel runs, I measured the actuator's forward thrust in still air at four spacings, three trials each.

40 mm gave the most thrust, 3.3 ± 0.2 mN, and was used for every test after. At 35 mm the gap sparked intermittently. Each spark wastes the discharge and threw large spikes into the load cell readings; those spikes were excluded from the average.

# Method

Every test ran at 11 m/s, about Re 60,000. At each angle of attack — 0°, 4°, 8°, 12°, 16°, 18°, 20° and 24° — I set the angle through the web interface, tared the load cells, started the fan, and waited for the forces to settle. Then I logged 10 seconds with the actuator off, followed straight away by 10 seconds with it on. The fan and actuator were then switched off for about a minute so the transformer would not overheat.

Comparing off and on within the same run, seconds apart, means slow drift in the tunnel or the load cells mostly cancels out.

Measured drag fell the most at shallow angles: 43% at 0° and 28% at 4°, shrinking steadily to about 1% at 24°. That trend fits where drag comes from. At low angles most of it is skin friction in the boundary layer, which the ionic wind acts on directly. At high angles most of it is pressure drag from the separated wake, which it barely touches.

## Separating Thrust from Drag

The actuator pushes forward by itself, which the spacing test measured at 3.3 mN in still air. The balance cannot tell that push apart from a real reduction in aerodynamic drag, so the drop above is an *apparent* drag reduction.

The thrust measurement gives a rough split. At 0° and 4° drag fell by 7.1 and 5.8 mN, about twice the actuator's thrust, so part of the drop there is an aerodynamic effect on the boundary layer. From 12° to 24° the drop matches the thrust within the measurement error, so at those angles it may be the actuator's push alone.

## Stall

Stall did not move. With the actuator off or on, lift levels off from 18° while drag climbs steeply, so the critical angle stayed at 18–20° either way.

The raw readings show the change of flow state. At 18° the lift trace is smooth; at 20° it swings by tens of millinewtons, the signature of separated, unsteady flow shedding vortices off the wing.

The hypothesis was only partly right. The ionic wind carries too little momentum to hold the flow on the wing near stall, and as the angle rises the separation point moves forward, ahead of the actuator, where its push no longer reaches the flow that is separating. This matches earlier work showing ionic wind is most effective while the flow is still attached. In this configuration the actuator works as a lift and efficiency booster, not a replacement for flaps.

# Limitations

- **Thrust and drag are mixed.** The balance measures the net force, so the actuator's own thrust cannot be separated from the aerodynamic effect except by the estimate above.
- **No power measurement.** Without the electrical power, there is no way to say whether the aerodynamic gain outweighs the energy spent.
- **One run per angle.** Each value comes from one off/on run; the readings within it are consecutive samples, not independent repeats.
- **The mount adds drag.** It was measured and subtracted, but only at 0°; at other angles its drag may differ slightly from what was subtracted.
- **Wall gaps and blockage.** There is about 5 mm between the wingtips and the walls, so some air leaks around the tips, and at high angles the wing blocks more of the test section and speeds up the flow.
- **Humidity was not recorded.** It is known to change how a corona discharge behaves.

# Next Steps

More power, or an actuator closer to the leading edge, are the most direct things to try for stall. Beyond that: measure the electrical power to settle the efficiency question; add endplates at the wingtips; and vary voltage, electrode position and airspeed together in a designed experiment instead of one factor at a time.
