# Flight Computer
A custom flight computer PCB was not used for this prototype as I was still not certain of the exact components and requirements, though I will likely make one for future versions. Instead, I used breakouts soldered to a perfboard for replaceability and developmental simplicity.

My flight computer was built around the Teensy 4.1, chosen for its clock speed, allowing for fast control loops and math, along with soldered-on PSRAM and flash for datalogging. [State estimation](gnc) and [communication](communications) were handled by the IMU, barometer, and LoRa radio (see [table below](#sensor-table)).

In the future, I will likely switch to an LSM6DSOX or ISM330DHCX IMU for lower drift than my current setup. I will also switch to a BMP581 for its ~3 cm altitude precision compared to my current ~50 cm precision.

Putting the power monitors on their own I2C bus is the deliberate part. A blocking transfer or a stretched clock on a shared bus delays everything behind it, and the barometer feeds the altitude estimate — a stall there shows up as an altitude glitch. Separating them means a slow power read cannot ever hold up a control-relevant sensor.

The GPS is on a hardware UART for the same reason. Early bench testing with the u-blox library showed parsing occasionally blocking for up to 6000 µs — long enough to drop three full control cycles. Re-running the same test on a separate microcontroller as a diagnostic gave 38 µs mean with 1.7 µs standard deviation, which confirmed the spikes were a software and interface problem rather than the receiver being slow.

# PCB Design
These were the first PCBs I've designed and my first opportunity to solder SMD components. I decided to make the PCBs out of the necessity for compact and reliable electronics as well as for the fun challenge and new skills. I made a power distribution board and a servo distribution board.

Both PCBs were designed in EasyEDA for its interface with JLCPCB for manufacturing. 5 boards of each were ordered for a total of just $6. The 0805 metric was chosen for components as it is compact but large enough to be comfortably hand-soldered with a soldering iron. I used screw terminals and JST connectors for easy connection and disconnection of components.

## Power Distribution Board
The avionics run on a dedicated 2S LiPo, entirely separate from the 6S LiPo powering the EDF. This split exists because the EDF motor draws high current under load, which would sag or inject noise onto a shared rail—putting the flight computer, IMU, and radio on their own isolated battery keeps sensor and logic power clean regardless of what the motor is doing.

The power distribution board was designed to distribute and convert power from the 2S LiPo, serving as the common ground for all sensors, servos, and the flight computer. It included two LM2940 low dropout regulators to convert the 2S LiPo voltage to 5V and 3.3V for the flight computer and sensors. Two toggle switches were used since they are satisfying, one for all power and one for just the servos. An INA260 chip was used to monitor power consumption and battery voltage.

## Servo Distribution Board
The servo distribution board was simple as it just needed to distribute power and signals to each servo. The board was designed to be as compact as possible while still being hand-solderable with an iron. It included ground and power planes for better current handling (up to 5A worst case) and noise reduction, with a 470 $\mathrm{\mu F}$ low ESR capacitor to smooth the power delivery. I also included 10 $\mathrm{\mu F}$ capacitors on each servo's power line to reduce noise and 330 $\Omega$ resistors on each signal line for protection and to reduce ringing.

## Where the IMU Sits

The IMU is not at the centre of mass. It sits about 0.4 m forward of it along the roll axis, because that is where there was room for it.

That offset has a consequence most builds ignore. An accelerometer at a lever arm from the centre of rotation does not measure the vehicle's linear acceleration — it measures that plus the acceleration produced by the vehicle rotating about its own centre. Pitch the vehicle and the IMU swings through an arc, reporting acceleration that the vehicle as a whole is not experiencing. Feed that straight into a velocity estimate and rotation is silently integrated into translation.

The offsets are stored in the flight code and the centripetal term $\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r})$ is subtracted before the reading reaches the estimator, so what the filter sees is much closer to acceleration at the centre of mass than at the sensor.

The tangential term $\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \times \mathbf{r}$ is *not* corrected, because it needs angular acceleration and differentiating the gyro adds more noise than the term removes at these rates. It is the smaller of the two in sustained rotation and the larger during a sharp transient, so this is a deliberate trade rather than an oversight — but it is a gap.

# Accel/Mag calibration
Plotting accelerometer and magnetometer readings in 3D as the sensor is rotated through all orientations should trace a sphere: radius 1 g for the accelerometer, ~50 µT (Earth's field at this location) for the magnetometer. In practice, sensor errors distort this sphere into an offset ellipsoid.

Accelerometers require correction for scale (a constant multiplier), bias (a constant offset), and cross-axis sensitivity (each axis not being truly independent from the others). Magnetometers require correction for hard iron offsets (fixed fields from nearby magnetized materials) and soft iron distortions (caused by induced fields in nearby ferromagnetic materials). Because both error models for the accelerometer and magnetometer distort a sphere into an ellipsoid, both sensors can be corrected with the same equation:
$$\mathbf{M'=A(M-b)}$$
where $\mathbf{M'}$ and $\mathbf{M}$ are the corrected and raw measurement vectors, and $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are the correction matrix and bias vector, transforming the offset ellipsoid into a centered sphere.

**Procedure:** The IMU was held stationary in several orientations spanning the full range of attitudes. Recorded data was filtered and downsampled to help mitigate orientation clustering from biasing the least-squares ellipsoid fit. MATLAB's `magcal` function then computed the best-fit ellipsoid and returned $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and the radius of the corrected sphere for both sensors.

### Results stored in flight code

// [ax ay az] = [A]([ax' ay' az'] - [b])      corrects for accel calibration and mag distortion 
const float A_acc[3][3] =   {{1.0055f, 0.0f, -0.0005f}, 
							{0.0f, 1.0038f, -0.00075f},

							{0.00269f,	0.04195f,	1.0369f}};

const float b_acc[3] = {-2.241559f, 15.39148f, 16.4980f};

## Judging a Calibration

The first magnetometer calibration was not good enough, and how that was determined matters more than the fix.

A calibration hands back a matrix and a bias vector. Whether it is any good is judged by applying it and checking that the corrected magnitude is constant — the Earth's field strength should not depend on which way the sensor happens to be pointing. Two things were tracked: the standard deviation of $|\mathbf{m}|$ as a fraction of its mean, and the *shape* of the residual distribution.

Location dominated the result. Calibrating indoors near a laptop gave 3%, then 7% of mean on a second attempt. Moving outside and away from the laptop brought it to **0.9%** on the third.

The distribution shape is the sharper diagnostic of the two. After a bad calibration the residuals were skewed with a heavy tail — the signature of structure the ellipsoid fit failed to absorb, meaning real distortion is still present. After a good one they were close to normal, which is what remains when the only error left is sensor noise. A normal residual is evidence that the error model was the right one; a skewed residual says the model has not finished its job no matter how small the standard deviation looks. Coverage was checked as well: 93.1% of 72 orientation bins occupied, so the fit was not extrapolating across an unsampled patch of the sphere.

## A Bug Hiding in a Convention

For a long time the Y and Z IMU axes appeared inverted, and the flight code carried a sign flip to compensate.

That flip produced correct behaviour, which is why it survived. It was still wrong — it was correcting for something that was not happening. The axes were fine. The *ground station* was displaying them wrong: it interpreted the attitude quaternion as an earth-to-body rotation when the flight computer was sending body-to-earth, and that mistake inverts precisely those axes. The original axis assignment had been made by reading that display, so a bug in the ground station had propagated backwards into the vehicle's coordinate conventions and then been patched over on the vehicle side.

# Magnetic Interference

The magnetometer sits about 12 cm from the nearest motor wiring, which carries up to 30 A. Before flying with it, the obvious question was whether that current would swamp the Earth's field.

Modelling the wire as an infinite straight conductor:

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(30)}{2\pi(0.12)} \approx 50~\mu\mathrm{T}$$

That is the same magnitude as the Earth's field at this location. If true, heading would be unusable at any appreciable throttle.

Measured data showed almost no interference at all. Comparing magnetometer readings from a flight against a reference recording with the EDF off, the difference was on the order of the reference's own fluctuation.

The prediction was wrong because the model was wrong. The motor wiring does not run as one long straight conductor past the sensor — it doubles back, forming a roughly triangular loop only about 1.5 cm across. At 12 cm away that is $R \ll d$, which is the dipole limit, not the infinite-wire limit. Outgoing and return currents very nearly cancel, and the field falls off as $1/d^3$ rather than $1/d$:

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2 d^3} \approx 2.5~\mu\mathrm{T}$$

Twenty times smaller, and squarely in line with what was observed.

The useful part was not the number. It was that a confident prediction failed, and the reason was a geometric assumption imported without checking whether it applied. The wire was never infinite; it only had to be long compared to 12 cm, and it is not.

The magnetometer is nonetheless not currently in the loop. Heading initialization is still unsolved — before recalibration the heading error reached 15° — so the vehicle flies without it and takes its heading reference elsewhere.

# GPS Characterization

The receiver is a u-blox NEO-M9N, used for Doppler velocity rather than raw position. Three things were established on the bench before it was trusted in the estimator.

## The Default That Was Silently Wrong

The receiver ships in a *pedestrian* dynamic model. That model embeds assumptions about plausible speed and acceleration, and it filters the solution accordingly — assumptions which are wrong for a vehicle that accelerates vertically.

## The Noise Is Not Gaussian

Velocity noise was characterized over roughly 8300 static samples. The standard deviation is small — around 0.02 m/s at best — but the distributions have visibly **heavy tails**: they look normal in the middle and produce far more large excursions than a Gaussian would.

This matters directly downstream. A Kalman filter assumes Gaussian measurement noise, and a heavy-tailed distribution means occasional outliers many sigma out. Tuning the filter's measurement covariance from the standard deviation alone would leave it over-trusting exactly those samples. Knowing the tails are there is what justifies gating the fusion rather than accepting every fix.

## Testing Its Own Interference
