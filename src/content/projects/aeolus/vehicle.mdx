# Airframe & Structure

The airframe was divided into two sections: the aft section (aft of the intake) and the forward section (forward of the intake). The aft section needed to accomodate the EDF and control mechanism, and was designed to be supported purely through 3D printed parts due to the rather tight space and complex geometry. The forward section held the batteries, ESC, and avionics, and was designed to have 4 carbon fiber rods acting as the main structural support with a thin 3D printed shell around it. The CF rods give it good strength and minimal weight compared to an entirely 3D printed structure.

Since this was a first prototype, all 3D printed parts were made from regular PLA due to its high stiffness, relatively good strength, and low cost. Future versions will likely use PC for its tensile strength, impact resistance, and stiffness while still being relatively affordable.

# Moment of Inertia Testing
## Inertia Tensor

The moment of inertia is the rotational analog to mass. It determines body dynamics, like how the angular velocity changes under a given moment. Thus, accurately knowing the moment of inertia is important for developing an accurate simulation to tune controller gains. The full moment of inertia is a tensor that is represented as a 3x3 matrix, not a simple scalar like mass. This is because the angular momentum and the angular velocity do not necessarily point in the same direction, unlike the linear analog where linear momentum points along the velocity.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
L_x \\
L_y \\
L_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_{xx} & I_{xy} & I_{xz} \\
I_{yx} & I_{yy} & I_{yz} \\
I_{zx} & I_{zy} & I_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_x \\
\omega_y \\
\omega_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

However, for the rocket where the axes are along the symmetry lines, each component of angular momentum does point along the corresponding angular velocity component, and the calculations are greatly simplified.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
L_x \\
L_y \\
L_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_{xx} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & I_{yy} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & I_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_x \\
\omega_y \\
\omega_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
L_x = I_{xx} \omega_x \\
L_y = I_{yy} \omega_y \\
L_z = I_{zz} \omega_z
$$

Due to the approximate cylindrically symmetric mass distribution, the pitch and yaw moments of inertia are approximately equal, so only 2 measurements need to be taken: the roll moment of inertia, $I_{xx}$, and the pitch/yaw moment of inertia $I_{yy/zz}$.

## Bi-filar Pendulum Setup

To measure the moments of inertia, a pendulum setup can be used. The most common way to do this is with a bi-filar pendulum setup, as it minimizes the propagated error [compared to something like a standard pendulum](#standard-pendulum-comparison).

The rocket is suspended on two strings (length $l$) equidistant from the center of mass (distance $d$). The period of small rotational oscillations can be measured and used to calculate the MOI according to the formula given below.

$$
I = \frac{T^2 m g d^2}{4 \pi^2 L}
$$

The measurement uncertainty in this setup $\delta I$ can be simply calculated by summing the relative uncertainties in $T, d, L$ in quadrature, approximating them as gaussian.

$$
\delta I = I \sqrt{(2 \delta d / d)^2 + (2 \delta T / T)^2 + (\delta L / L)^2}
$$

Compared to the standard pendulum method below, the propagated uncertainties in the variables are significantly smaller.

<Dropdown title="Standard Pendulum">
### Standard Pendulum

<img src="/assets/aeolus/moi/Screenshot_2026-06-10_at_4.19.02_PM.png" alt="diagram" width="40%" class = "mx-auto dark:invert brightness-105 dark:brightness-100"/>

For a standard pendulum setup, the MOI about the CM is given below (from $T=2\pi\sqrt{I/mgd}$ and the parallel axis theorem).

$$
I = \frac{T^2 m g d}{4\pi^2} - m d^2
$$

The uncertainty can be calculated by taking partial derivatives w.r.t. to $T$ and $d$ and summing in quadrature.

<img 
    src="/assets/aeolus/moi/Screenshot_2026-06-10_at_4.39.55_PM.png" 
    alt="Uncertainty Propogation" 
    style={{ maxWidth: '70%', borderRadius: '8px' }}
    class = "mx-auto dark:invert brightness-105 dark:brightness-100"
/>

$$
\delta I_{sp} = \sqrt{(I + m d^2)^2 \left( \frac{2 \delta T}{T} \right)^2 + (I - m d^2)^2 \left( \frac{\delta d}{d} \right)^2}
$$

Since $m d^2 \gg I$ this is usually not a viable method to measure the MOI since propagated errors are huge. If instead $d$ was reduced, $T$ would decrease and $\delta T/T$ would increase; in short $\delta I$ cannot be made arbitrarily small.
</Dropdown>
