# Airframe & Structure

The airframe was divided into two sections: the aft section (aft of the intake) and the forward section (forward of the intake). The aft section needed to accommodate the EDF and control mechanism, and was designed to be supported purely through 3D printed parts due to the rather tight space and complex geometry. The forward section held the batteries, ESC, and avionics, and was designed to have 4 carbon fiber rods acting as the main structural support with a thin 3D printed shell around it. The CF rods give it good strength and minimal weight compared to an entirely 3D printed structure.

The split is really a split in **load path**. Aft of the intake, thrust and vane reaction loads are fed into printed structure directly, because the geometry there is too congested for rods to run through cleanly. Forward of the intake, the four CF rods carry essentially all axial load, and the printed shell around them is an aerodynamic fairing rather than a structural member — which is what lets it be thin. Mass in the forward section is also the mass furthest from the centre of mass, so it is the mass that costs the most pitch and yaw inertia. Keeping the shell non-structural was as much a control decision as a weight one.

Since this was a first prototype, all 3D printed parts were made from regular PLA due to its high stiffness, relatively good strength, and low cost. Future versions will likely use PC for its tensile strength, impact resistance, and stiffness while still being relatively affordable.

The structure got an unplanned test on 27 August, when a position-hold flight ended in the vehicle's first crash. Inspection afterwards found no damage — a useful data point for a prototype airframe printed in a material chosen mostly for cost.

## Canopy Sizing

Descent rate follows from a force balance at terminal velocity:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho A C_d}}$$

The interesting term is $A C_d$, not $A$ alone. A toroidal canopy runs a drag coefficient near $2.2$ against roughly $0.75$ for a flat canopy, so it buys the same drag from far less fabric. Sizing both to a comparable descent rate makes the gap concrete:

A 0.7 m toroid reaches 5.44 m/s on 0.37 m² of canopy. A flat octagon needs roughly 1.15 m across and about 1.09 m² for the same descent rate — **three times the fabric for the same result**. In the nose, where every gram sits at the worst possible station for pitch and yaw inertia, and where packing volume is tightest, that ratio is what decided the design.

The fabric column is left blank for the toroid deliberately: projected area sets drag, but a toroidal canopy is a three-dimensional surface whose *total* fabric area is meaningfully larger than its projection, so multiplying through by areal density would understate it. The pattern generator reports both numbers separately for exactly this reason.

## Shock Cord Characterization

A shock cord is not a spring. It is stiff at low strain, soft through the middle of its range, and then stiffens hard as the fibers lock up — and the opening load depends entirely on where in that curve the cord ends up. So it was measured rather than assumed, by stretching samples against a meter stick and reading tension off a force scale.

The complication is that stiffness $k = F/\delta l$ depends on sample length, so results from different pieces of cord cannot be compared directly. Normalizing by the unstretched length removes that dependence:

$$S = k L_0 = EA$$

$S$ is a property of the material and cross-section, not of the particular sample. If the measurement is sound, samples of different length must collapse onto a single $S(\lambda)$ curve — which is exactly what happened:

That collapse is what convinced me the measurement was sound. It says the numbers describe the cord itself rather than one arbitrary piece of it, which is what makes them safe to use as model inputs. Across the full range $S$ falls from about 121 at $\lambda = 1.17$, bottoms out near 52 around $\lambda = 2.0$, and climbs back to roughly 76 by $\lambda = 2.6$ — the stiff, soft, then strain-stiffening shape expected of an elastomer.

One honest limitation: the shortest samples carry the largest relative error, since a few millimetres of meter-stick uncertainty on a 50 mm elongation is several percent. The high-stiffness point anchoring the low-strain end is the least trustworthy number in the set.

## Opening Shock Model

Peak cord tension is what sizes the shock cord attachment, and it cannot be read off a static pull test — it depends on how the rocket and canopy decelerate against each other. So the deployment transient was simulated as a genuine two-body problem: rocket and canopy as separate masses, coupled only through the cord, integrated with Euler–Cromer at 0.1 ms steps.

The measured cord data enters as a fitted force law, slack below $\lambda = 1$ and asymptotic at maximum extension:

$$F(\lambda) = A(\lambda - 1)^{p} + \frac{B(\lambda - 1)}{\lambda_{max} - \lambda}$$

with $A = 35.5$, $B = 12.6$, $p = 0.4$, $\lambda_{max} = 2.8$. Through the mid-range the fit tracks the bench data to within about 4%; it loosens past $\lambda \approx 2.4$, where the asymptotic term turns sharply and the measurements thin out.

The canopy side carries one term that is easy to miss. A parachute does not just accelerate its own fabric — it drags a substantial column of air with it, and that entrained air dominates the canopy's effective inertia:

$$m_{added} = \frac{4}{3}\pi\rho r^3, \qquad r = \sqrt{A/\pi}$$

For this canopy that comes to 0.646 kg against 50 g of fabric, so the entrained air is roughly **fourteen times** the mass of the parachute itself. Leave it out and the opening load is underpredicted by a factor of three.

For the sizing case — a 1.6 kg vehicle deploying at 20 m/s — the model returns a peak cord tension of **330 N** and a peak vehicle deceleration of **20 g**, both about 120 ms after the cord goes taut. That peak tension is the load case behind the [shock cord attachment FEA](#cad--mechanical-design) above, which closes the loop: bench measurement feeds the material model, the material model feeds the deployment simulation, and the simulation sets the load the part is designed against.

That number carries an honest caveat. At peak load the cord reaches $\lambda = 2.72$, past the highest point on the bench (2.6) and deep into the region where the asymptotic term dominates and nothing has been measured. The design load is therefore an extrapolation, not an interpolation. Pulling the cord to $\lambda \approx 2.75$ — close to failure, on a sample that can be sacrificed — is the single measurement that would most improve confidence in the recovery system, and it is the next thing to do if the parachute is reinstated.

# Moment of Inertia Testing
## Inertia Tensor

The moment of inertia is the rotational analog to mass. It determines body dynamics, like how the angular velocity changes under a given moment. Thus, accurately knowing the moment of inertia is important for developing an accurate simulation to tune controller gains. The full moment of inertia is a tensor that is represented as a 3x3 matrix, not a simple scalar like mass. This is because the angular momentum and the angular velocity do not necessarily point in the same direction, unlike the linear analog where linear momentum points along the velocity.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
L_x \\
L_y \\
L_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_{xx} & I_{xy} & I_{xz} \\
I_{yx} & I_{yy} & I_{yz} \\
I_{zx} & I_{zy} & I_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_x \\
\omega_y \\
\omega_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

However, for the rocket where the axes are along the symmetry lines, each component of angular momentum does point along the corresponding angular velocity component, and the calculations are greatly simplified.

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
L_x \\
L_y \\
L_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_{xx} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & I_{yy} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & I_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\omega_x \\
\omega_y \\
\omega_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
L_x = I_{xx} \omega_x \\
L_y = I_{yy} \omega_y \\
L_z = I_{zz} \omega_z
$$

Due to the approximate cylindrically symmetric mass distribution, the pitch and yaw moments of inertia are approximately equal, so only 2 measurements need to be taken: the roll moment of inertia, $I_{xx}$, and the pitch/yaw moment of inertia $I_{yy/zz}$.

## Bi-filar Pendulum Setup

To measure the moments of inertia, a pendulum setup can be used. The most common way to do this is with a bi-filar pendulum setup, as it minimizes the propagated error [compared to something like a standard pendulum](#standard-pendulum-comparison).

$$
I = \frac{T^2 m g d^2}{4 \pi^2 L}
$$

The measurement uncertainty in this setup $\delta I$ can be simply calculated by summing the relative uncertainties in $T, d, L$ in quadrature, approximating them as gaussian.

$$
\delta I = I \sqrt{(2 \delta d / d)^2 + (2 \delta T / T)^2 + (\delta L / L)^2}
$$

Compared to the standard pendulum method below, the propagated uncertainties in the variables are significantly smaller.

<Dropdown title="Standard Pendulum">
### Standard Pendulum

<img src="/assets/aeolus/moi/Screenshot_2026-06-10_at_4.19.02_PM.png" alt="Standard pendulum geometry diagram" width="40%" class = "mx-auto dark:invert brightness-105 dark:brightness-100" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />

For a standard pendulum setup, the MOI about the CM is given below (from $T=2\pi\sqrt{I/mgd}$ and the parallel axis theorem).

$$
I = \frac{T^2 m g d}{4\pi^2} - m d^2
$$

The uncertainty can be calculated by taking partial derivatives w.r.t. to $T$ and $d$ and summing in quadrature.

<img 
    src="/assets/aeolus/moi/Screenshot_2026-06-10_at_4.39.55_PM.png"

$$
\delta I_{sp} = \sqrt{(I + m d^2)^2 \left( \frac{2 \delta T}{T} \right)^2 + (I - m d^2)^2 \left( \frac{\delta d}{d} \right)^2}
$$

Since $m d^2 \gg I$ this is usually not a viable method to measure the MOI since propagated errors are huge. If instead $d$ was reduced, $T$ would decrease and $\delta T/T$ would increase; in short $\delta I$ cannot be made arbitrarily small.
</Dropdown>

## EDF Rotor Inertia, Without Measuring It

The bifilar pendulum measures the MOI of the entire vehicle. However, the MOI of the EDF rotor is useful as it determines the gyroscopic moments induced during pitching maneuvers and the roll moment during startup/spindown.

When the EDF spins up while the vehicle hangs freely on the tether, there is no significant external torque about the roll axis, so angular momentum about that axis conserved. The rotor accelerates one way and the airframe rotates the other. Since $I_{edf} \ll I_{rkt}$:

$$
I_{rkt}\,\omega_{rkt} = I_{edf}\,\omega_{edf}
$$

During roll testing the airframe reached roughly $11~\mathrm{rad/s}$ while the EDF peaked near $29{,}000$ rpm, or about $3040~\mathrm{rad/s}$. With $I_{xx}$ already measured:

$$
I_{edf} \approx \frac{I_{rkt}\,\omega_{rkt}}{\omega_{edf}} \approx \boxed{6\times10^{-6}~\mathrm{kg\,m^2}}
$$

This is a rough estimate. The biggest issue is that $\omega_{rkt}$ was only estimated, as the vanes were producing torque in the opposite direction throught the entire spinup.
