# Vane Design

The new vanes were characterized the same way as the old ones, which makes the comparison direct:

Pitch and yaw scaled with area exactly as expected. Roll did not — it gained only a third more authority from twice the vane.

I do not have a confirmed explanation. The most plausible one is that roll is the axis where the vanes interfere with each other most: a roll command deflects all four vanes in the same rotational sense, so each one sits in the swirl shed by its neighbours, while a pitch or yaw command only works a single opposing pair. Larger vanes in a confined jet would make that interaction worse, and it would show up on roll alone — which is what the data does. That is a hypothesis, not a result; separating it from a moment-arm or dynamic-pressure-distribution effect would take CFD on the full four-vane set, or the stand test proposed on the [testing page](/projects/aeolus/testing#roll-authority-did-not-scale-with-vane-area): measure roll authority with two opposing vanes removed.

It matters because roll authority is what the vane change was meant to fix. The original vanes took about three seconds to null the startup roll transient, which is slow enough to eat into a short flight.

# Measuring Control Authority

Control authority was estimated three ways: CFD, the thrust stand, and fits to flight data. They do not agree ([comparison below](#comparing-the-three-estimates)). The controller uses the flight fit, because it describes the vehicle as it actually flies.

## Static — Thrust Stand

<Dropdown title='Vane Moment Testing'>
The thrust vane testing was conducted with the 4-bar load cell cross test stand using the force differential across load cell pairs to calculate the moment and approximate the lateral force on each vane. Aeolus has 4 thrust vanes, each a half-ellipse (optimal shape to minimize induced drag) with span (semi-major axis) of 25 mm and chord (minor axis) of 20 mm. Each is connected to an axle offset but linked to the external fin axles, controlled via a servo.

Due to poor design, force was not spread evenly across each load cell when pure axial thrust load was applied due to rigidity issues and different preloading from uneven geometry. Each load cell was held down with a weight, but the heights were not perfectly even, and nor were the angles perfect level, so some of the wooden planks faces did not perfectly interface with the bricks, causing uneven loading. When load was applied, the forces would distribute unevenly between load cells due the structures bending differently from the different preloads.

A baseline test was run at 70% throttle to get baseline forces at 0 degree fin deflection (which were not spread evenly due to the issues above). A test was also run with 1 Nm pitching moment applied with hand-held force probe, again exhibiting same non-even force and cross talk issues.

The rig was compromised by uneven preload and cross-talk between cells (details in the dropdown above). Rather than discard the run, the measurement was salvaged by referencing it against a known moment. A 1 N·m pitching moment was applied by hand with a force probe to establish the differential that a known load produces; every subsequent measurement was then read as a ratio against that reference. The absolute per-cell readings stay wrong, but the ratio cancels a scale error in each reading as long as the rig responds linearly. It does not remove the cross-talk or uneven bending, and the hand-applied reference has its own uncertainty, so these numbers are less certain than the load cells' resolution suggests.

Surprisingly, the measured moments at 70% throttle correlated closely with published flat-plate $C_l$ data at comparable Reynolds number. The vane cross-section is a very thin airfoil, which turns out to be near enough to a flat plate.

## Dynamic — From Flight Data

The bench gives a static number under an idealised load. The vehicle in flight gives the number that actually matters, and it can be extracted without any test equipment at all.

Commanded vane deflection and measured angular acceleration are both already in the telemetry log. Fitting one against the other with RANSAC — chosen because logged flight data has outliers that would drag an ordinary least-squares line — gives a slope in rad/s² per degree. Multiplying by the [measured inertia](/projects/aeolus/vehicle#moment-of-inertia-testing) converts it straight to torque:

$$
\frac{\tau}{\mathrm{deg}} = I \cdot \frac{d\alpha}{d\delta}
$$

For roll, a slope of about 1.5 deg⁻¹s⁻² against $I_{xx} = 0.00165$ gives 0.0025 N·m/deg. For pitch and yaw, 0.1 deg⁻¹s⁻² against $I_{yy} = 0.065$ gives 0.0065 N·m/deg. Both are the v1 figures in the table above.

## Trim, For Free

The fitted line does not pass through the origin. That offset is not noise — it is the deflection required to produce *zero* angular acceleration, which is exactly the definition of trim. The x-intercept hands over a calibration constant that would otherwise have to be dialled in by trial and error, from data collected for a different purpose.

Physically it reflects small asymmetries: geometry that is not quite symmetric, vanes that are not quite aligned, a thrust axis fractionally off the centre of mass. The values flown now are 3.0° roll, −0.35° pitch, and 0.9° yaw, re-measured after the vane change. They are added to the PID output rather than left for the integrator to discover, so the controller starts from the right place instead of winding its way there.

# Deriving the Gains

Because vane authority had been fitted [from flight data](#dynamic--from-flight-data) as angular acceleration per degree, the attitude gains could be set from a target response instead of found by trial and error. They were a starting point: the roll gains were lowered after an oscillation on the 4 September flights. Each axis is a rotational mass with a restoring term and a damping term — a damped harmonic oscillator:

$$
I\ddot{\theta} + K_d\dot{\theta} + K_p\theta = 0
$$

So rather than guessing gains and watching what happens, you choose the *response you want* — how fast it should correct, and how much it should overshoot — and solve backwards for the gains that produce it.

<DampingDemo />

## Solving the Oscillator

That equation is standard, but the two quantities the whole method rests on — $\omega$ and $\zeta$ — are not free definitions. They fall out of solving it, and the solve is worth showing because it is where the design knobs come from.

Substituting the ansatz $\theta(t) = Ae^{i\omega t}$ and dividing through by the exponential leaves a quadratic in $\omega$:

$$
\begin{gathered}K_p + K_D\,i\omega - I\omega^2 = 0 \\[4pt] \Longrightarrow \quad \omega = \frac{iK_D}{2I} \pm \sqrt{\frac{K_p}{I} - \left(\frac{K_D}{2I}\right)^2}\end{gathered}
$$

The root has a fixed imaginary part and a square root that may be either real or imaginary. Writing the general solution as a combination of both roots separates the two behaviours:

$$
\begin{gathered}\theta(t) = e^{-\gamma t}\left(Ae^{i\Omega t} + Be^{-i\Omega t}\right) \\[6pt] \gamma = \frac{K_D}{2I}, \qquad \Omega = \sqrt{\frac{K_p}{I} - \left(\frac{K_D}{2I}\right)^2}\end{gathered}
$$

$\gamma$ is the decay envelope and $\Omega$ is what happens inside it. The interesting case is the boundary where $\Omega = 0$ — critical damping, the fastest return with no overshoot, which occurs when

$$
K_p = \frac{K_D^2}{4I}
$$

That boundary is what makes the damping ratio a natural variable rather than an invented one. Defining

$$
\zeta^2 = \frac{K_D^2}{4IK_p}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{K_p}{I}}
$$

puts critical damping at exactly $\zeta = 1$, so $\zeta$ measures damping *as a fraction of critical* — and $\omega$, the undamped natural frequency, sets the timescale. Inverting the two definitions gives the gains in terms of the response:

$$
K_p = I\omega^2, \qquad K_D = 2\zeta I\omega
$$

Two measurable quantities go in and two design choices come out. $\zeta$ is chosen for the damping shape wanted; $\omega$ is chosen for how fast the vehicle can actually correct, which is bounded by [control authority](#measuring-control-authority). Settling time follows from the same substitution — the envelope decays with time constant

$$
\tau \sim \frac{1}{\gamma} = \frac{2I}{K_D} = \frac{1}{\zeta\omega}
$$

so the measured authority and a chosen settling time set the starting dynamics before anything is flown.

### The Step Response

Solving for the constants completes the picture. Releasing the vehicle from an initial offset with zero rate — $\theta(0) = \theta_0$, $\dot\theta(0) = 0$ — gives $A + B = \theta_0$ and $-\gamma\theta_0 + i\Omega(A - B) = 0$, and the complex exponentials collapse back into real trigonometric functions:

$$
\theta(t) = \theta_0\,e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\Omega t + \frac{\gamma}{\Omega}\sin\Omega t\right)
$$

In terms of the design variables, $\gamma = \zeta\omega$ and $\Omega = \omega\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, so the ratio in front of the sine is just $\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$. Everything the vehicle will do after a disturbance is contained in those two numbers.

## The Gains Are the Design Targets

There is an implementation choice that makes this unusually clean. The attitude PID does not output a torque or a vane angle. It outputs an **angular acceleration**, which is converted to vane deflection afterwards using the measured torque per degree. Because $\alpha = \tau/I$, the inertia divides out of the gain expressions entirely, and they collapse to:

$$
K_p = \omega^2, \qquad K_d = 2\zeta\omega
$$

The consequence is that the flight code's gain table is readable as design intent rather than as tuning residue. Reading `config.h` backwards:

## Saturation Set by Physics

The output limits come from the same measurements. The most angular acceleration the vanes can produce is the deflection budget allocated to an axis, times the torque per degree, divided by the inertia:

$$
\alpha_{max} = \frac{\delta_{max}\cdot(\tau/\mathrm{deg})}{I}
$$

which in the flight code is written out literally as `(servo deg × τ/deg) / MOI`. Roll gets 6° of the 27° available and reaches 12 rad/s²; pitch and yaw get 17° each and reach 3.4 rad/s². The controller therefore cannot command an acceleration the vehicle is incapable of producing — the saturation limit *is* the physical control authority, not an arbitrary clamp.

The lopsided deflection budget follows directly from the inertia tensor. Roll inertia is forty times smaller than pitch and yaw, so a few degrees of vane buys far more angular acceleration about that axis. Spending 17° on roll would be wasted travel.

## Against Reality

Roll behaved as the model said it would. The flown configuration sits at $\zeta = 0.83$ and the measured response tracked the second-order prediction closely enough that the two are hard to tell apart.

Pitch and yaw were tuned the same way to the same target and came out slightly *less* damped in flight than predicted — a real discrepancy, most likely because their authority figure was less well pinned down at the time. The trim angles the same analysis predicted, however, were spot on.

## Knowing When Not To Use It

The velocity loop was tuned by a different method, deliberately. There, the controller takes velocity error and **outputs acceleration** — so the controller's output is the *first* derivative of the controlled variable, not the second. Writing the PD loop out makes the difference explicit:

$$
-K_p v - K_d\dot{v} = \dot{v} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad K_p v + (K_d + 1)\dot{v} = 0
$$

The commanded acceleration *is* $\dot{v}$, so the loop closes one derivative lower than the attitude loop does, and there is no $\ddot{v}$ term for a mass to resist. This is a first-order equation, and a first-order equation cannot oscillate. There is no natural frequency to place and no damping ratio to choose; the solution is a pure exponential decay:

$$
v(t) = v_0 e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{K_d + 1}{K_p}
$$

So the target was a settling time instead. A 2% settling time of one second is roughly $4\tau$, which set the starting gains at $K_p = 4.0$ with $K_d = 0$ — no derivative term at all, because in this model there is nothing to damp, and $K_d$ only slows the response down. The model leaves out the attitude loop's lag, though: the vehicle takes time to reach the tilt the velocity loop asks for. In free flight that lag caused an oscillation, and a derivative gain of about 0.5 is what removed it ([Simulation](/projects/aeolus/simulation#used-predictively-not-just-descriptively)).

## What It Rests On

The whole approach rests on one measurement: control authority, the angular acceleration one degree of vane produces.

Because authority was fitted from flight data in angular-acceleration units, the measured inertia cancels out of the attitude loop: torque per degree is the fitted slope times $I$, and the controller divides by $I$ again when it turns a commanded acceleration into vane degrees. Inertia only matters when authority comes from a torque measurement, as on the thrust stand or in CFD.

An error in authority by a factor $k$ scales the effective $K_p$ and $K_d$ by $k$. Since $\omega = \sqrt{K_p}$ and $\zeta = K_d/(2\sqrt{K_p})$, both $\omega$ and $\zeta$ shift by $\sqrt{k}$: a 20% error gives about a 10% change in damping, not none. Authority also sets the saturation limit, so an error there means the controller believes it has authority it does not have. That is why authority was measured by several unrelated methods, and why the pitch and yaw axes, whose figure was the less settled at the time, are the ones that came out off-target in flight.

# Attitude Controller

## Loop Structure

Control runs as a cascade, each layer slower than the one it feeds:

```
position  (8 Hz)  →  velocity  (25 Hz)  →  attitude  (200 Hz)  →  vane mixing  →  4 servos
altitude PID      →  thrust           →  RPM setpoint          →  governor     →  ESC
```

The outer loops are slow because they only need to nudge a setpoint; the attitude loop is fast because attitude error leaks directly into position error. Guidance is not allowed unlimited say over attitude — the commanded tilt is clamped to 10°, so a confused position loop cannot ask for an attitude the vehicle should not be in.

## Quaternion Error and the 180° Problem

Attitude error is computed as a quaternion difference rather than as Euler angles, which avoids gimbal lock and keeps the error well-defined at large angles. It introduces a different problem.

A quaternion and its negation represent the same orientation, so error extracted naively flips sign as the vehicle passes through 180°. In roll testing this showed up concretely: the controller reversed direction past 180°, which meant the high roll rates present at spin-up were driven the wrong way instead of being arrested. It was found in flight data on 22 July and fixed the next day.

That was not the end of it. The July fix was the shortest-path sign pick itself. It stops the controller taking the long way round, but because the sign is chosen afresh every tick, a fast spin through 180° flips the command instantly. That is what happened in September: the logged error (which is $2\sin(\theta/2)$, so it tops out at 114.6°) flipped sign in a single tick as the true error crossed 180°, and briefly drove the spin instead of arresting it. Full diagnosis on the testing page: [the unwinding bug](/projects/aeolus/testing#the-unwinding-bug).

The fix ties into a second choice visible in the [PID implementation](#pid-controller): the attitude loop uses a second `update()` overload that takes the measured gyro rate directly, so the derivative term is $-K_d \cdot \dot{\theta}_{measured}$ rather than a numerical difference of the error signal. Taking the derivative on the measurement instead of on the error avoids derivative kick when the setpoint steps, and it sidesteps the sign discontinuity entirely — the gyro does not care how the error is parameterised.

## Mixing and Saturation

Roll, pitch, and yaw commands are combined additively across the four vanes. In the original code, if the summed command exceeded the mechanical limit the vanes clipped, and *none* of the three requested moments was delivered faithfully: a large roll command could use up the authority that pitch and yaw were counting on. The allocator now scales all three together instead ([below](#saturating-without-rotating-the-torque)).

This was one of three candidate explanations for the first tethered flight losing attitude on 19 June, alongside mis-set gains and the nonlinear vane force curve.

## Thrust Compensation

Two corrections keep the altitude loop honest while the vanes are working:

**Vane drag.** Deflected vanes should block the jet and cost axial thrust, so commanded thrust is boosted in proportion to total deflection beyond a deadband — the deadband stopping small deflections around hover from making the correction chatter.

The 40 m flight log does not support this. The measured thrust-versus-deflection slope came out *positive*, and about five times the coded magnitude — the wrong sign for a loss at all. The correction is therefore inflating commanded thrust to compensate for something that is not in the data. Closed-loop that is harmless, since the altitude PID absorbs the offset; open-loop it is not, because it makes every thrust budget built on top of it — the [relight solve](/projects/aeolus/gnc#the-relight-solve) especially — optimistic. Unresolved.

**Tilt.** Only the vertical component of thrust fights gravity, so the commanded thrust is divided by $\cos(\text{tilt})$. It is computed straight from the quaternion components with no trigonometric calls, and floored at 0.8 so a large attitude excursion cannot drive the divisor toward zero and command infinite thrust.

## PID Controller

<Dropdown title='PID code'>
```cpp
class PID {
private:
    float kp, ki, kd;
    float integralClamp;        // Limits I to ±integralClamp
    float outputMin, outputMax; // Limits the final output
    float integral = 0.0f;
    float prevError = 0.0f;

    float P, I, D;

public:
    PID(float kp, float ki, float kd, float integralClamp, float outputMin, float outputMax)
        : kp(kp), ki(ki), kd(kd), 
          integralClamp(integralClamp), 
          outputMin(outputMin), outputMax(outputMax), 
          integral(0.0f), prevError(0.0f) {}

    float update(float error, float dt) {
        // Proportional
        P = kp * error;
        //  Integral
        integral += error * dt;
        I = ki * integral;
        I = constrain(I, -integralClamp, integralClamp);
        integral = (ki != 0.0f) ? I / ki : 0.0f;
        // Derivative
        float derivative = (dt > 0.0f) ? (error - prevError) / dt : 0.0f;
        D = kd * derivative;
        prevError = error;
        // output
        float out = P + I + D;
        out = constrain(out, outputMin, outputMax);
        return out;
    }
    //for gyro rate + euler error
    float update(float error, float dt, float measuredRate) {
        // Proportional
        P = kp * error;

        // Integral (unchanged)
        integral += error * dt;
        I = ki * integral;
        I = constrain(I, -integralClamp, integralClamp);
        integral = (ki != 0.0f) ? I / ki : 0.0f;

        // Derivative
        D = -kd * measuredRate;

        float out = P + I + D;
        out = constrain(out, outputMin, outputMax);
        prevError = error; // kept for reset()/compatibility, unused in derivative now
        return out;
    }

    void reset() { 
        integral = 0.0f; 
        prevError = 0.0f; 
    }

    // Dynamic tuning update method 
    void setGains(float Kp, float Ki, float Kd) {
        kp = Kp;
        ki = Ki;
        kd = Kd;
    }

    void setGains(float Kp, float Ki, float Kd, float I_clamp) {
        kp = Kp;
        ki = Ki;
        kd = Kd;
        integralClamp = I_clamp;
    }

    float getP(){ return P;}
    float getI(){ return I;}
    float getD(){ return D;}
};
```
</Dropdown>

The anti-windup is done by back-calculating the integral from its clamped contribution rather than clamping the accumulator directly — `integral = I / ki` after `I` has been constrained. This keeps the stored integral consistent with the output actually delivered, so the term unwinds immediately when the error reverses instead of having to burn off accumulated history first.

# Control Allocation

The attitude controller asks for a torque. Something has to turn that into four servo angles, and the conversion factor is not a constant — it changes continuously through the flight.

## Authority Is Recomputed Every Tick

Both effectors on a shaft contribute, from different physics:

$$
\begin{aligned}k_{net} = {} & \underbrace{K_{vane} \cdot \text{(vane drive)}}_{\propto\ \text{thrust}} \\ & + \underbrace{K_{aero} \cdot \bar{q} \cdot \text{flow sign}}_{\propto\ \text{airspeed}^2}\end{aligned}
$$

The vane term scales with thrust, which moves with throttle, battery voltage and how far through a spool-up the fan happens to be. The aero term scales with dynamic pressure, which moves with speed. So the deflection needed to produce a given torque is different at every moment, and the allocator computes $k_{net}$ fresh each cycle before dividing the demanded torque by it.

One detail matters more than it looks: the thrust estimate comes from **measured** RPM, not commanded RPM. During a spool-up the governor may be asking for 38,000 while the tachometer reads 24,000, and the authority the vehicle actually has is whatever the tachometer says. Using the command would have the controller confidently under-deflecting exactly during the transient where it needs the most help.

## When the Two Effectors Fight

Because vane and fin share a shaft, their moments do not always add.

In base-first descent, ram air pushes back through the duct against the fan's flow. The allocator models this directly — vane drive is thrust minus that backflow momentum — and when the backflow wins, **vane drive goes negative and the vane's moment reverses**. The vane now deflects with the fin rather than against it. The two only genuinely oppose in powered descent, where the fan is pushing one way and the freestream the other.

Near the crossover their contributions nearly cancel, and the *sign* of the net authority becomes unknowable. Deflecting on a sign you cannot determine is worse than not deflecting: get it wrong and the correction becomes a disturbance. So there is a null guard — if the two terms oppose and their magnitudes are within ±15% of each other, that axis parks at neutral until the ambiguity resolves.

## No Authority Is Different From Weak Authority

Below a floor value of $k_{net}$ there is not weak authority — there is none. Both effectors are inert, and the full 27° of deflection would produce about $2.7\times10^{-5}$ N·m, which is nothing.

This code originally slammed to the mechanical stop in that case, on the reasoning that weak authority deserves maximum deflection. That reasoning is correct when $k_{net}$ is small but non-zero, and in that regime the division saturates on its own anyway. It is wrong at exactly zero — which turns out to be the *normal* condition on the pad, because `LAUNCH` holds zero throttle until the target RPM ramps up to the fan's minimum, roughly 0.4 s during which thrust and dynamic pressure are both zero.

The result was that every single launch drove all four surfaces hard to ±27° on gyro noise for that window, before the vehicle had left the ground. It now parks at neutral instead.

## Trim as a Torque, Not an Angle

The [trim offsets](#trim-for-free) are stored as torque per newton of thrust rather than as fixed angles. Trim comes from real disturbances — exhaust swirl, a thrust axis slightly off the centre of mass — and those scale with thrust. Dividing that torque by $k_{net}$, which also scales with thrust, leaves the resulting *angle* constant in still air, which is what the measurement actually showed.

## Saturating Without Rotating the Torque

The four servo commands are formed as $-\text{roll} \pm \text{pitch}$ or $\pm\text{yaw}$, so clamping each axis independently would let one servo clip while the others do not — which does not just reduce the torque, it points it somewhere else.

Instead the allocator finds the peak servo command and, if it exceeds the mechanical limit, scales all three axes by the same factor. The commanded torque loses magnitude but keeps its direction. The scale factor is reported in telemetry, so a sustained value below 1 is visible after the flight as "the gains are asking for more authority than the vanes have."

## What Is Still Untested

Honest status: the aerodynamic half of this has barely been exercised. At the speeds flown so far the vane dominates so heavily that `K_AERO`, the flow-sign logic and the null guard have effectively never been tested in flight — the null guard has yet to arm at all.

Two things make that tolerable. There is a written flight test plan with predicted values and stop criteria for exactly these signals, so the first flight that does exercise them will be instrumented for it. And in the hover regime that *has* been flown extensively, the allocator is arithmetically identical to the fixed-constant code it replaced — so the refactor reduces to the same arithmetic as the previously validated code everywhere the vehicle has actually been.
