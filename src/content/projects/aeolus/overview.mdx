# Design Narrative

Most of what's below lives in more detail on the subsystem pages linked
throughout — this page is the argument, not the evidence. It's the
reasoning chain that connects one physics problem to every decision that
followed from it.

## 1. The Physics Problem

An EDF rocket has no wings, no fins doing meaningful work at zero forward
velocity, and no aerodynamic restoring moment until the vehicle is already
moving fast enough for its surfaces to bite into the air. On the pad, and
for the first fraction of a second off it, the vehicle is not a rocket in
the aerodynamically stable sense — it's an inverted pendulum balanced on a
thrust vector, and every disturbance torque has to be cancelled by active
control, because nothing else is going to cancel it.

$$
\tau_{control} = I\alpha + \tau_{disturbance}
$$

That equation looks trivial. It isn't, because $I$ and $\tau_{disturbance}$
aren't constants you get to assume — they're the outputs of every
mechanical decision made on [the vehicle page](/projects/aeolus/vehicle),
and $\tau_{control}$ is bounded by whatever the [thrust vanes](/projects/aeolus/control)
can actually deliver. The control problem doesn't start in the flight
code. It starts here, and the code exists to solve what this equation
demands.

## 2. Why the Obvious Approaches Don't Work

Three places where the straightforward version of a solution failed, and
what that failure taught me:

**Vane sizing.** The intuitive move is to size thrust vanes by eye,
tune gains until the response looks reasonable, and move on. I did size
them that way first. Bench testing showed the resulting force curve was
badly nonlinear across the deflection range — not because the geometry
was wrong in some subtle way, but because the vanes are fully stalled
across their entire operating range at the Reynolds numbers involved
(Re ≈ 58,500). The nonlinearity wasn't a control problem to tune around.
It was an aerodynamics problem that tuning couldn't fix. Full derivation
and bench data on [the control page](/projects/aeolus/control).

**Sensor fusion.** The obvious way to estimate position is to fuse GPS
position directly with IMU-integrated position. I built that first, too.
The problem is that direct position fusion lets GPS noise show up
directly in the state estimate — over a 30-second flight, that's
meaningful positional uncertainty. Fusing GPS *Doppler velocity* instead,
and integrating that, reduces the random-walk growth by roughly 30x over
the same window, because velocity noise integrates far more gracefully
than position noise propagates. The harder problem this exposed: roll has
no equivalent correction source. Gravity gives you pitch and roll... no,
gravity gives you no roll reference at all around the vehicle's vertical
axis, and the magnetometer — the obvious fallback — degrades exactly when
you need it most, under EDF motor current. The fix uses the current
sensor itself as the trust signal for the magnetometer, gating it down
dynamically as current rises, since current (not throttle percentage) is
the physically correct proxy for the field distortion. Full architecture
on [the state estimation page](/projects/aeolus/state-estimation).

**Quaternion error.** The standard small-angle approximation for
quaternion attitude error, $2\sin(\theta/2)$, works fine near zero error
and compresses badly as error grows — which is exactly the regime a
disturbed vehicle is in. Roll errors near 180° produced control outputs
that made no physical sense until I switched to an $\text{atan2}$-based
error formulation and added explicit hysteresis around the antipodal
sign discontinuity in the quaternion representation. That discontinuity
isn't a magnitude problem — it's a structural property of $SO(3)$, and it
corrupts all three axes simultaneously when it triggers, not just roll.
Detail on [the control page](/projects/aeolus/control).

## 3. The Coupled Tradeoff

None of the subsystems on this project are independent, and treating them
as independent is the mistake that costs the most time. The clearest
example: vane geometry, structural load, vehicle mass, and control gains
form a closed loop.

Larger vanes generate more control authority — but they also see higher
aerodynamic loads, which demands more structural material to survive
those loads without failing, which adds mass, which raises the moment of
inertia the control system has to fight, which increases how much control
authority is actually needed to hit a given angular acceleration target.
Sizing the vanes without accounting for the mass and inertia consequences
of that sizing decision produces a vehicle that's *harder* to control than
a smaller-vane design, not easier — because the authority gain gets eaten
by the inertia increase.

$$
\alpha_{max} = \frac{F_{y,max} \cdot d_{vane}}{I_{yy}}
$$

This is why [the Rev 2 proposals](/projects/aeolus/testing#next-steps)
touch mass relocation and vane resizing together rather than either one
alone — changing one side of that equation without the other doesn't move
the number that actually matters.

The control authority ceiling above is currently a hand-calculated /
CFD-derived prediction, not a measured, flight-verified number — the
vanes haven't yet been characterized on a thrust stand to get real
$dM/d\delta$ data, and until that happens the number stays labeled as a

Testing status and next steps are documented in full on
[the testing page](/projects/aeolus/testing), including what's blocking
progression to the next test phase and what the plan is to clear it.

## 5. Why This, and Why Aerospace

The part of this project that's kept me in it isn't any single subsystem
— it's that none of them can be solved in isolation. A control theory
problem turns into a structural mass problem turns into an aerodynamics
problem turns back into a control theory problem. That's the actual shape
of aerospace engineering as a discipline, and it's a different kind of
problem than the single-variable optimization you get in most physics
coursework. Working through it here — with hardware that actually breaks
when the math is wrong — is what confirmed aerospace over a narrower,
single-discipline path.
