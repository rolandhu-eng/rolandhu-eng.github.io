# Overview
The flight computer has three main jobs: it must determine its current orientation and location (navigation), determine how to get to its desired orientation and position (guidance), and control its hardware to get there (control).

This page covers the first two, plus the flight state machine that sequences the mission. The [control](/projects/aeolus/control) page covers the third.

# Flight Software Structure

The flight code is about 5,300 lines of C++ on the Teensy, split into modules that each own one job. There is no RTOS: one loop runs as fast as it can, and each task fires when its own timer comes due.

## One Control Tick

Every 5 ms (200 Hz) the main loop runs the same sequence, in this order:

1. **Read sensors.** The IMU is read every tick. The barometer (every 20 ms), power monitors (15 ms) and GPS (as messages arrive) are read only when new data is due, so a slow sensor never holds up the tick.
2. **Estimate the state.** Attitude, then earth-frame acceleration, then velocity and position, with barometer and GPS corrections applied when a new sample is in.
3. **Compute control authority.** The allocator works out what one degree of vane is worth right now, from *measured* RPM and the current airflow.
4. **Run the state machine.** It decides the flight state, which control loops are switched on, and how thrust is set.
5. **Set thrust.** Off, idle, maximum, or governed by the altitude loop, depending on the state.
6. **Run the outer loops when due.** Position every 120 ms, velocity every 40 ms. Each produces a new setpoint for the loop below it.
7. **Run attitude and allocation.** The attitude loop asks for an angular acceleration, the allocator turns it into four servo angles, and the servos are driven.
8. **Command the fan.** Thrust becomes an RPM setpoint for the governor.
9. **Log the tick.** Sensors, state, controller internals and allocator state go into the PSRAM buffer.

The order matters. Authority is computed before the state machine and the attitude loop because both need it, and the log is written last so each record describes the tick that just ran. Anything slow, like flushing the log to flash, waits until the vehicle is back in `IDLE` on the ground.

# State Estimation

To control itself, the rocket must first know where it is and how it is currently moving. To do this, it has an IMU that measures the linear acceleration and the angular rates. The velocity, position, and orientation are obtained by integrating the acceleration and angular rate. However, there are some complications. These measurements are relative to the rocket itself (body frame), so they must be rotated to get measurements in the Earth frame. Furthermore, an accelerometer cannot distinguish between gravity and uniform acceleration (equivalence principle in General Relativity), so the gravity vector must be first subtracted from accelerometer readings. Both of these operations require accurate orientation; any small error in the measured orientation will cause the gravity vector to "leak" into the true acceleration, causing huge drift. An small angular error $\theta$ will cause roughly $g\theta$ of acceleration error; over time, this results in $\frac{1}{2}g\theta t^2$ of position error. A $1^\circ$ error over $10~\text{s}$ results in $8.6~\text{m}$ of position drift and $1.7~\text{m/s}$ of velocity drift. Thus, accurate orientation is of paramount concern.

That single calculation sets the priorities for everything below. It is why the attitude filter runs at 200 Hz while the position loop runs at 8 Hz, why the [IMU lever arm](/projects/aeolus/avionics#where-the-imu-sits) is compensated, and why the horizontal channel is given ten times the process noise of the vertical one.

## Frame Convention

Aeolus uses an **X-up body frame**: the roll axis, X, points along the vehicle's length and therefore vertically when it is standing on the pad. Sitting level, the accelerometer reads $+1$ g on X and zero on Y and Z.

This is the natural choice for a rocket — the axis of symmetry is the axis you care about, and it keeps the roll axis first — but it is not the convention most reference implementations assume, and that had consequences downstream.

## Rotating Between Frames

Every accelerometer and gyro sample arrives in the body frame. Velocity, position, and every guidance target live in the earth frame. The bridge between them is a rotation, and it was derived from scratch rather than lifted, because the X-up convention above changes which terms survive.

Going from body axes back to earth axes means undoing three elementary rotations in order — yaw $\gamma$, then pitch $\beta$, then roll $\alpha$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ 0 & \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\beta & 0 & \sin\beta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma & 0 \\ \sin\gamma & \cos\gamma & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying the three out gives the direction cosine matrix, body to earth:

$$
R = \begin{bmatrix}
\cos\beta\cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma\cos\beta & \sin\beta \\
\sin\alpha\sin\beta\cos\gamma + \cos\alpha\sin\gamma & -\sin\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma + \cos\alpha\cos\gamma & -\sin\alpha\cos\beta \\
-\cos\alpha\sin\beta\cos\gamma + \sin\alpha\sin\gamma & \cos\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma + \sin\alpha\cos\gamma & \cos\alpha\cos\beta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The flight code does not build this matrix in the fast path. Rotating a vector by a quaternion directly is cheaper — nine multiplies and no trigonometry at all — and it avoids the gimbal singularity that the Euler parameterisation carries at $\beta = 90^\circ$, which for an X-up rocket is a horizontal attitude rather than an exotic one:

```cpp
// t = 2 * cross(q_vec, v);  v_earth = v + qw*t + cross(q_vec, t)
float tx = 2.0f * (qy*vz - qz*vy);
...
v_earth[0] = vx + qw*tx + (qy*tz - qz*ty);
```

The DCM still earns its place. It is the form in which a rotation can be *read* — each entry is the cosine of the angle between one body axis and one earth axis — and that is what makes it the right tool for a fixed, small, measured rotation. It is used directly to correct the [IMU's mounting misalignment](/projects/aeolus/avionics#mounting-misalignment).

## Madgwick Filter

Integrating the gyro alone gives orientation, but only briefly. Gyro bias is never exactly zero, so integration accumulates it without bound and the estimate walks away. The accelerometer provides an absolute reference — gravity always points down — but it is useless on its own, because it cannot tell gravity from the vehicle's own acceleration and it is drowned in vibration from a fan spinning at 35,000 rpm.

Madgwick's filter fuses the two by treating attitude correction as an optimisation. It defines an objective function measuring the mismatch between the gravity direction the accelerometer reports and the gravity direction the current attitude estimate predicts:

$$
f(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{a}) = R(\mathbf{q})\,\hat{\mathbf{g}} - \hat{\mathbf{a}}
$$

then takes a single gradient-descent step against that error each cycle, blending it with the gyro integration. The gain $\beta$ sets how hard it leans on the accelerometer versus the gyro.

### Why the Standard Derivation Did Not Apply

The published algorithm — and every reference implementation of it — assumes gravity lies along **Z**. That assumption is not a parameter; it is baked in. Because $\hat{\mathbf{g}} = [0, 0, 1]^{T}$ is a constant, the authors expand $f$ and its Jacobian $J$ analytically and simplify by hand, dropping the terms that multiply the two zero components. What ships is a page of algebra with the Z-up assumption already dissolved into it.

With X as the vertical axis, $\hat{\mathbf{g}} = [1, 0, 0]^{T}$. Different components survive, different terms drop, and the simplified expressions no longer describe the right objective. The gradient $\nabla f = J^{T} f$ has to be re-derived from the objective function for the new reference direction — not translated, not axis-swapped after the fact, but rebuilt from the point where the assumption enters.

<Dropdown title="Show the math: the X-up objective and gradient">

With $\hat{\mathbf{g}} = [1, 0, 0]^T$, the direction the current attitude estimate expects gravity to appear in the body frame is the first row of the body-to-earth rotation matrix. Subtracting the measured, normalised acceleration gives the objective:

$$
f(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{a}) =
\begin{bmatrix}
q_0^2 + q_1^2 - q_2^2 - q_3^2 - a_x \\
2(q_1 q_2 - q_0 q_3) - a_y \\
2(q_1 q_3 + q_0 q_2) - a_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

For comparison, the published Z-up objective is

$$
f_{Z\text{-up}} =
\begin{bmatrix}
2(q_1 q_3 - q_0 q_2) - a_x \\
2(q_0 q_1 + q_2 q_3) - a_y \\
2(\tfrac{1}{2} - q_1^2 - q_2^2) - a_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Every row is different.

Differentiating each row with respect to $q_0 \ldots q_3$ gives the Jacobian:

$$
J =
\begin{bmatrix}
2q_0 & 2q_1 & -2q_2 & -2q_3 \\
-2q_3 & 2q_2 & 2q_1 & -2q_0 \\
2q_2 & 2q_3 & 2q_0 & 2q_1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and the gradient $\nabla f = J^T f$ is what the code computes, term by term:

```cpp
s0 =  2*q0*f1 - 2*q3*f2 + 2*q2*f3;
s1 =  2*q1*f1 + 2*q2*f2 + 2*q3*f3;
s2 = -2*q2*f1 + 2*q1*f2 + 2*q0*f3;
s3 = -2*q3*f1 - 2*q0*f2 + 2*q1*f3;
```

Each step, the gyro rate is integrated and one normalised gradient step is subtracted, then the quaternion is renormalised:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{q} \otimes [0, \boldsymbol{\omega}] \;-\; \beta \frac{\nabla f}{\lVert \nabla f \rVert}
$$

</Dropdown>

Rotating the sensor frame to fake a Z-up convention would have been the shortcut. It also would have put the numerically-privileged axis in the wrong place and left every downstream frame — control, guidance, telemetry — disagreeing with the physical vehicle.

$\beta = 0.04$ in flight. It is a trust parameter: raise it and the filter corrects gyro drift faster but admits more vibration and manoeuvre acceleration into the attitude estimate; lower it and the attitude is smooth but drifts. On a vehicle whose accelerometer sits in the vibration field of an EDF, the accelerometer earns less trust than it would on a quieter platform.

### The Fast Inverse Square Root

The reference implementation normalizes vectors with the famous `0x5f3759df` bit-hack — an integer approximation of $1/\sqrt{x}$ from an era when floating-point division was expensive.

It was replaced with a plain `1.0f / sqrtf(x)` after it showed instability. On a Teensy 4.1 the substitution costs nothing measurable: the Cortex-M7 has a hardware floating-point unit with a square-root instruction, so the trick optimises away a problem the processor no longer has. The trick was solving a problem this processor does not have.

The replacement is guarded, and the guard is argued from physics rather than defensiveness:

```cpp
if (x <= 1e-10f) {
    return 0.0f;
}
return 1.0f / sqrtf(x);
```

Every call site passes a sum of squares, so $x$ is non-negative by construction and the only dangerous input is zero. In the gradient-normalisation call, a near-zero gradient magnitude means the filter has converged — the estimate already matches the measurement. Returning zero there is not a safe fallback papering over a division by zero; it is the *physically correct* answer, because the correct correction to apply is none.

## Integrating the Rates

With attitude known and gravity removed, what remains is earth-frame acceleration — and the states the controller actually needs are velocity and position. Getting from one to the other is integration, performed 200 times a second on a signal that never stops changing.

The obvious approach holds acceleration constant across the timestep. It is also wrong in a way that does not average out: the accelerometer reports a value at the *end* of each step, so treating that value as if it applied across the whole step systematically credits the vehicle with acceleration it had not yet reached.

Assuming instead that acceleration varies linearly between samples — the previous reading $a'$ and the current one $a$ — gives

$$
a(t) = a' + \left(\frac{a - a'}{\Delta t}\right)t
$$

Integrating once returns the trapezoidal sum, which is the familiar result:

$$
\begin{aligned}v &= v' + \int_0^{\Delta t}\left[a' + \frac{a-a'}{\Delta t}t\right]dt \\ &= v' + \left(\frac{a' + a}{2}\right)\Delta t\end{aligned}
$$

Integrating a second time is where it gets interesting:

$$
\begin{aligned}x &= x' + \int_0^{\Delta t}\left[v' + a't + \frac{a-a'}{2\Delta t}t^2\right]dt \\ &= x' + v'\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}a'\Delta t^2 + \frac{a-a'}{6}\Delta t^2\end{aligned}
$$

$$
x = x' + v'\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{6}\left(2a' + a\right)\Delta t^2
$$

</figure>

That last coefficient is the point of doing the derivation rather than assembling the answer from parts. The tempting shortcut is to take the trapezoidal average acceleration $\bar{a} = (a'+a)/2$, drop it into the constant-acceleration formula $x = x' + v'\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\bar{a}\Delta t^2$, and get $\tfrac{1}{4}(a'+a)\Delta t^2$ — which weights the two samples equally. The correct term weights the old sample **twice** as heavily as the new one, because under linearly changing acceleration the vehicle spends the whole step near $a'$ and only reaches $a$ at the final instant. Averaging first and integrating second is not the same operation as integrating.

```cpp
// x = x' + v'*dt + 1/6*(a + 2a')*dt^2
state.position_m[0] += vx_old*dt + (1.0f/6.0f)*(state.accel_ms2[0] + 2.0f*ax_old)*dt*dt;

// v = v' + 1/2*(a + a')*dt
state.velocity_ms[0] += 0.5f*(state.accel_ms2[0] + ax_old)*dt;
```

Neither correction is large in a single 5 ms step. Both matter because of what the [error analysis above](#state-estimation) established: dead reckoning has no memory of being wrong, so anything that biases a step in a consistent direction integrates into real drift. A first-order method is biased whenever acceleration is changing, and on a vehicle under active control acceleration is always changing. Trapezoidal integration removes that bias for the cost of keeping one previous sample in memory.

# Vertical Channel

Altitude and vertical velocity fuse the barometer with the accelerometer. The barometer is absolute but slow and noisy at roughly ±0.5 m; integrated acceleration is smooth and fast but drifts without bound. Each covers the other's weakness.

In the code this is two separate corrections on top of the integrated accelerometer.

**Altitude is pulled toward the barometer.** Each new barometer sample (50 Hz) moves the altitude estimate 1% of the way toward the barometer reading:

$$
h \leftarrow h + K_1\,(h_{baro} - h), \qquad K_1 = 0.01
$$

At 50 Hz that is a time constant of about $1/(50 \times 0.01) = 2$ s. Short barometer noise is smoothed out, and a steady barometer offset is followed within a few seconds.

**Vertical velocity is corrected by GPS.** GPS Doppler velocity goes through the same one-dimensional Kalman filter as the [horizontal channel](#gps-fusion), with the measurement noise taken from the receiver's reported speed accuracy on each fix.

GPS altitude is not used anywhere. That mattered on the 40 m flight: when the barometer read several metres low during the descent, the altitude estimate followed it within a couple of seconds and nothing else could pull it back. [Teardown on the testing page](/projects/aeolus/testing#it-was-the-barometer-not-the-controller).

# Horizontal Channel

Horizontal position has no equivalent of the barometer. Nothing on board measures it directly, so dead reckoning from the accelerometer is the only source between GPS fixes — and per the error analysis above, that is precisely the channel where attitude error leaks in as $g\theta$.

Process noise sets how quickly the filter's confidence in its own propagation decays between corrections. Allowing horizontal uncertainty to grow ten times faster means the filter is correspondingly readier to accept a GPS correction when one arrives, which is the right posture given how quickly horizontal dead reckoning goes wrong.

## GPS Fusion

GPS velocity is fused with IMU-integrated velocity through a one-dimensional Kalman filter per axis. This fixed both velocity and position drift, since position is the integral of the corrected velocity rather than an independently estimated state.

<Dropdown title="Show the math: the velocity Kalman filter">

The IMU integration carries velocity forward between GPS fixes, so the filter only has to track how uncertain that velocity has become. For each axis, the variance $P$ grows with time since the last fix:

$$
P^- = P + Q\,\Delta t
$$

When a fix arrives, the gain decides how far to move toward the GPS reading:

$$
\begin{gathered}
K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} \\[4pt]
v \leftarrow v + K\,(v_{gps} - v), \qquad P = (1 - K)\,P^-
\end{gathered}
$$

$Q$ is 0.002 m²/s³ vertically and 0.02 horizontally ([process noise, above](#horizontal-channel)). $R$ is not fixed: it comes from the speed-accuracy estimate $s$ the receiver reports with every fix, as $R_v = s^2/12$ vertically and $R_h = s^2/8.33$ horizontally. A long gap since the last fix makes $P^-$ large and $K$ close to 1, so the GPS is trusted. A fix the receiver itself reports as poor makes $R$ large and $K$ small, so it barely moves the estimate. The term in brackets, $v_{gps} - v$, is the innovation that gets logged.

</Dropdown>

The receiver's **Doppler velocity** is used as the correction, not its position solution. Doppler velocity is derived from carrier frequency shift rather than from code-phase ranging, and it is both more accurate and less prone to the slow wandering that afflicts position fixes under multipath. For a vehicle that needs to hold station rather than know its global coordinates, velocity is also the more directly useful observable.

The filter's **innovation** — the difference between the predicted and measured velocity — is logged on all three axes. That is instrumentation added specifically to debug the filter: innovation is where fusion problems become visible, since a filter that is over-trusting, under-trusting, or being fed outliers shows it in the innovation sequence long before the position estimate visibly fails.

## Heading Is the Open Problem

Roll about the vertical axis has no gravity reference. The accelerometer pins two degrees of freedom — it can tell which way is down — but rotation about the down vector leaves it unchanged. Resolving that requires either a magnetometer or motion.

The magnetometer is [calibrated and characterized](/projects/aeolus/avionics#magnetic-interference), and the motor turned out not to disturb it meaningfully. But heading accuracy before recalibration reached 15° of error, and initialising heading reliably is unsolved. The vehicle currently flies without it.

# Guidance

Guidance converts a position target into an attitude target through two cascaded loops.

```
position error  →  [P, 8 Hz]   →  velocity target
velocity error  →  [PD, 25 Hz] →  acceleration command
acceleration    →  tilt + bearing  →  target quaternion
```

A tilted vehicle accelerates horizontally because its thrust vector tips. So a commanded horizontal acceleration becomes a commanded tilt:

$$
\text{tilt} = \arcsin\left(\frac{|\mathbf{a}_{cmd}|}{F_{tilt}/m}\right)
$$

where $F_{tilt}$ is the sideways force available per radian of tilt: measured thrust, plus the body's aerodynamic side force, which helps nose-first and opposes base-first. The argument is clamped to $[-1, 1]$ before the `arcsin` rather than trusting the loop above to produce something physical, and the loop holds instead of tilting when there is too little force to lean on, or when thrust and aerodynamic force are close and opposed so the direction of the result is unknowable. The bearing comes from `atan2` of the two acceleration components, and the pair are composed into a target quaternion.

<Dropdown title="Show the math: tilt and bearing to a target quaternion">

With bearing $\psi = \operatorname{atan2}(a_E, a_N)$, the vehicle must tip its up-axis toward the commanded direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}} = (0, \sin\psi, \cos\psi)$, written in (up, east, north). The rotation axis is perpendicular to both, $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{up} \times \hat{\mathbf{d}} = (0, -\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$, so the tilt quaternion is:

$$
\mathbf{q}_{tilt} = \left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; 0,\; -\cos\psi\,\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; \sin\psi\,\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)
$$

Roll about the vertical axis is kept separately. Since X is up, the roll part of the previous target is just its $q_0$ and $q_1$ terms, renormalised:

$$
\mathbf{q}_{roll} = \frac{(q_0,\, q_1,\, 0,\, 0)}{\sqrt{q_0^2 + q_1^2}}
$$

and the new target applies roll first, then tilt:

$$
\mathbf{q}_{target} = \mathbf{q}_{tilt} \otimes \mathbf{q}_{roll}
$$

Why the roll part comes from the previous *target* and not the measured attitude is explained [below](#holding-roll-while-tilting).

</Dropdown>

## Holding Roll While Tilting

Composing the tilt with the vehicle's current heading has a subtlety worth spelling out. The roll setpoint is extracted from the *previously commanded* target quaternion, not from the measured attitude.

Taking it from the measurement seems natural and is wrong. The velocity loop runs every cycle, and if each cycle rebuilt the target from wherever the vehicle currently is, the roll setpoint would be silently overwritten with the actual roll — which is to say the roll controller would be handed its own output as its reference and would stop correcting anything. Reading from the commanded target keeps the roll setpoint an independent quantity that the roll loop can actually track.

# Flight Sequencing

Everything above describes loops that hold a setpoint. Something has to decide what the setpoint *is*, and when to change it. That is a state machine running above the whole cascade, and since September it flies the entire profile with no input after the launch command.

```
IDLE → LAUNCH → BOOST_UP → APOGEE → [COAST_DOWN] → DESCENT → LANDING
```

Each state owns two things: which control loops are active, and what the thrust mode is (off, idle, governed, or max). `BOOST_UP` climbs on the altitude PID. `APOGEE` holds a 3 s hover at the target — deliberately, so the drop always starts from the same known condition rather than from whatever state the climb happened to end in. `DESCENT` tracks a computed arrest curve down to 0.3 m, and `LANDING` takes the last stretch at a fixed 0.1 m/s.

`ABORT` is reachable from every state. Two policy decisions in it are worth stating plainly, because both are cases where the obvious response is wrong:

**Link loss lands the vehicle. It never aborts.** Aborting cuts thrust, and a vehicle that has lost its radio is not a vehicle that has lost its ability to fly. Cutting the fan because the operator stopped talking would convert a communications fault into a crash.

**A sink the vehicle cannot arrest does not abort either.** When the descent rate exceeds what maximum thrust could still stop from the current height, the machine latches a `committed_` flag and keeps braking at full thrust. It cannot save the landing at that point, but every metre per second it removes before contact is a metre per second the airframe does not have to absorb. Giving up because the situation is unrecoverable makes it strictly worse. The fan is cut only below 0.4 m and only if the vehicle is still coming down faster than 0.8 m/s — that cut exists to prevent a rotor burst on impact, not to end the flight.

## The Relight Solve

The interesting state is the one that gets skipped.

`COAST_DOWN` drops the fan to idle and lets the vehicle fall ballistically, which is the whole point of a drop test. Restarting from idle is not instant: the fan takes about 1.5 s to spool, the vehicle keeps accelerating downward the entire time, and only then does braking begin. So before entering the coast the machine solves for the altitude at which it would have to relight:

$$
\begin{aligned}h_{relight} = {} & \underbrace{v_d t + \tfrac{t^2}{2}\left(g - \tfrac{T_{idle}}{m}\right) - \tfrac{t^2 (T_{max}-T_{idle})}{6m}}_{\text{fall during spool}} \\ & + \underbrace{\frac{v_{end}^2}{2 a_{dec}}}_{\text{braking}} \;+\; 2\,\mathrm{m}\end{aligned}
$$

The first group integrates a linear thrust ramp from idle to maximum over the spool time; the second is the distance needed to arrest the speed that ramp leaves you with. If that number is larger than the current altitude, there is no coast budget and `COAST_DOWN` is skipped entirely — the fan stays under the governor and the vehicle flies a powered descent instead. In practice that threshold sits near 19 m, so every flight below it descends under power whether or not a drop was requested.

The arrest curve itself is computed in energy form rather than as a fixed rate, so the descent speed limit falls smoothly to the landing rate exactly at the handover altitude instead of collapsing to zero and relying on a floor to catch it:

$$
v_{cap} = \min\left(\sqrt{v_{land}^2 + 2 a_{dec} h},\; 12~\mathrm{m/s}\right)
$$

## Idle That Cannot Windmill

`COAST_DOWN` does not shut the fan off. It runs a computed idle, and the reason is a failure mode specific to ducted fans falling base-first.

If the fan produces less thrust than the oncoming air's momentum flux, the flow through the duct reverses and the rotor windmills backwards. An ESC asked to restart a rotor that is already spinning the wrong way does so slowly and unreliably — which is exactly the wrong behaviour to discover at the bottom of a relight solve that assumed 1.5 s. So idle thrust is set to keep the duct flowing forward with margin:

$$
T_{idle} = \min\left(T_{hover},\; \max\left(T_{min},\; 1.5 \cdot 2 \rho A v^2\right)\right)
$$

floored at the ESC's minimum stable speed and capped at hover, since anything above hover would not be a coast.

## Events, Not Just Telemetry

Every transition, abort reason, forced landing, commit latch and touchdown writes a timestamped record into the flash log. Previously that context lived only in `Serial.printf`, which meant it existed only if somebody happened to have a console attached — and the flights that most need explaining are the ones nobody was tethered to.
