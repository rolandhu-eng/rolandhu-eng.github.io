# Overview

In my freshman engineering class I led a team of three to design and build a wind tunnel: a cheap, small tool for seeing and measuring how air moves around an object. A year later I turned it into a measurement instrument and used it, on my own, to answer a research question: can a wing be made more efficient with no moving parts, using a wire held at 40,000 volts?

# The Result

A thin copper wire near the front of the wing is held at up to 40 kV. It ionizes the air around it, and the ions are pulled toward a grounded brass tube further back, dragging air along with them. That "ionic wind" speeds up the slow layer of air right at the wing's surface.

- **Lift rose 5–13% at every angle tested.**
- **Measured drag fell most at shallow angles** — 43% at 0°, shrinking to about 1% at 24°. Part of that is the actuator's own forward push, which this setup could not separate from the aerodynamic effect; [the research page](/projects/wind-tunnel/research#separating-thrust-from-drag) works out how much.
- **At 8°, where the unpowered wing is most efficient, lift-to-drag rose 25%.**
- **Stall was not delayed.** It stayed at 18–20° with the actuator on or off. The ionic wind carries too little momentum to hold the flow on the wing near stall.

The tunnel made this possible because it measures forces, not just flow. Most published work on this kind of actuator studies how the flow moves; I wanted the forces on the wing, and that is what the [upgrades](/projects/wind-tunnel/upgrading) were for.
